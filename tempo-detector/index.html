Tempo basics

What a tempo detector measures

Tempo is the speed of a musical pulse. BPM turns that sensation into a useful number: 60 BPM is one beat per second, 120 BPM is two.

A browser tempo detector begins with decoded audio samples, not a title or database entry. It looks for events that often align with the beat: kick and snare attacks, claps, bass notes, chord changes, and other sudden rises in energy. These events are called onsets.

BPMDetector builds an onset envelope and checks how strongly it repeats at different intervals. A regular peak every half second supports 120 BPM. The winning interval becomes the estimate, while competing intervals affect the confidence message.

The process is statistical rather than magical. A detector does not know which event a musician considers “the beat.” It measures repetition and selects the most plausible musical interpretation. That is why a transparent confidence estimate and alternate half-time or double-time values are useful.

The analysis happens after Web Audio decodes your file locally. No microphone permission and no server upload are required.

Words and numbers

BPM vs. tempo: what is the difference?

Tempo is the musical idea of speed; BPM is the number used to measure it. They are closely related, but they are not interchangeable in every situation.

Tempo describes the feel

Musicians have long used words such as largo, andante, moderato, and allegro to communicate a general pace and character. Those markings leave room for interpretation. Two performances marked andante can move at different measured rates and still feel appropriate.

BPM is more precise. A project set to 128 BPM gives a DAW, delay plug-in, drum machine, or lighting system an exact timing reference. It also lets a DJ compare two tracks before attempting a transition.

The same BPM can feel different

A sparse 140 BPM trap beat may be felt in half-time at 70, while a busy 140 BPM drum-and-bass track emphasizes the faster pulse. A 100 BPM funk groove can feel more active than a dense rock song at the same number because accents and subdivisions shape perceived motion.

Use the automatic result as a measurement, then listen for the pulse that organizes the phrases. If the displayed number feels twice as fast or half as fast as your count, try the alternate interpretation rather than assuming the audio is wrong.

Know the limitations

Steady tempo, changing tempo, and rubato

When one BPM works

Electronic dance music, programmed pop, drum loops, and tracks recorded to a click usually have a stable tempo. A single BPM describes most of the track, and automatic detection can be highly confident.

When tempo moves

Live bands may drift naturally. Classical performances and solo passages may use rubato—deliberate expressive speeding and slowing. Film music can follow picture with a detailed tempo map. One result may then represent an average or the section with the clearest rhythm.

When the beat is ambiguous

Syncopated rhythms emphasize spaces between beats. Ambient music may lack sharp attacks. Odd meters organize pulses into groups that a basic detector does not label. In each case, the audio can support several mathematically reasonable answers.

How to verify

Listen to the section you intend to use and tap the beat you would count aloud. If your tap is near half or twice the automatic value, the detector probably found the same timing at a different metrical level.

Practical workflow

Why DJs check tempo before a mix

A tempo reading helps a DJ estimate how much adjustment a transition will require. Moving a 124 BPM track toward 126 BPM is usually a smaller decision than forcing an 88 BPM track into a 128 BPM set. The number can guide track order, but it does not choose the transition for you.

Start with a section that contains a clear, steady groove. Check whether the analyzer and your tap agree, then confirm the downbeat and phrase boundaries in your DJ software. Beat matching aligns recurring pulses; phrase matching decides when musical sections begin together.

Do not treat genre ranges as rules. Hip-hop often sits around 70–100 BPM, house commonly around 118–130, techno often around 120–140, and drum and bass frequently around 160–180, but real catalogs extend beyond those ranges. Some software also reports a half-time value, so a 174 BPM track may appear as 87.

Finally, check audio quality after changing speed. Key lock can preserve pitch, but large stretches may soften transients or introduce artifacts. A clean transition at a smaller adjustment is often better than matching two numbers at any cost.

Common mistake: comparing BPM labels without checking whether both tracks use the same half-time or double-time interpretation. Tap both tracks to the pulse you would actually mix.

Better source, better estimate

How to get a cleaner tempo reading

Automatic analysis works best when the section contains repeated rhythmic information. A few preparation choices can make the estimate easier to trust.

Do not chase decimal precision that the recording cannot support. A programmed loop may sit exactly on a grid, but a live drummer can move around the pulse and a vinyl transfer can drift slightly. For cueing, editing, or building a tempo map, decide whether you need one practical average or section-by-section timing before accepting the result.

01

Choose a steady section

Use a chorus, verse, or drop with established drums instead of a free-time intro, silent outro, breakdown, or isolated vocal. A longer regular passage gives the detector more repeated intervals to compare.

02

Use the clearest file

Prefer the original audio file when possible. Phone recordings, clipped exports, heavy crowd noise, and aggressive compression can blur transients. Converting a low-quality file to WAV does not restore timing detail that was already lost.

03

Check the musical count

Count at least eight beats aloud and tap along. Then compare your average with the automatic BPM and its half-time or double-time option. Agreement across both methods is stronger evidence than either number alone.

Tempo detector FAQ

Useful answers

What does a tempo detector measure?+

It estimates how often the main musical pulse repeats and expresses that rate as beats per minute.

Can a song change tempo?+

Yes. Live performances, classical music, film scores, and productions with tempo automation may speed up or slow down.

Why is tempo detection sometimes uncertain?+

Sparse percussion, syncopation, rubato, unusual meters, quiet transients, and half-time rhythms can support more than one plausible pulse.
